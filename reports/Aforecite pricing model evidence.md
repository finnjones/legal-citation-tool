# Literature backs the shape, not the outcome

The research literature **supports the overall shape of the Aforecite model, but it cannot confirm that the model will work**. Only experiments with real law students can do that. Three elements have solid support: per-essay pricing for a tool used a few times a year, guest checkout with no forced login, and a small prepaid pack sold alongside single essays. Three elements are reasonable but rest on thin or indirect evidence: the free 3-correction preview, the 14-day free re-run, and the emailed redeem code. Two parts of the earlier reasoning need correcting. First, **the 10-pack is not a "decoy"**. At A$3.50 per essay it beats the 3-pack's A$4.00, so any lift it produces comes from the compromise effect, which can also cannibalise the 3-pack. Second, **the strongest field evidence on tariff choice says anxious buyers prefer flat rates and happily overpay for them**, so a per-use-only line-up may leave money on the table. No study has tested this exact bundle for a low-frequency, high-stakes digital purchase. The evidence comes from telephone plans, gyms, catalogues, a book publisher and lab chocolate. **A note on sources:** no full-text article pages could be opened in this session. Claims tagged **[S]** come from search extracts (abstracts, publisher listings, secondary summaries). Claims tagged **[K]** come from model knowledge and were not checked this session. Every effect size below should be checked against the original paper before it is quoted externally, and [K] figures most of all.

## Per-use and guest checkout carry the strongest support

Per-essay pricing fits the purchase. Scribbr, the closest student-facing business, sells its plagiarism checker strictly per document in word-count tiers and says it offers no subscription ([Scribbr](https://www.scribbr.com/frequently-asked-questions/annual-subscription-plagiarism-checker/)). remove.bg keeps a pay-as-you-go option for users with uncertain volume ([remove.bg](https://www.remove.bg/help/a/what-is-the-difference-between-the-free-plan-subscription-and-pay-as-you-go)). The evidence against comes from the most rigorous field study. Lambrecht and Skiera's internet-provider data found **flat-rate bias "more important" and more persistent than pay-per-use bias**. It is driven by an insurance motive (avoiding a variable bill), a taxi-meter motive (disliking the meter running) and overestimation of use. It also **did not raise churn, while pay-per-use bias did** ([LBS](https://www.london.edu/faculty-and-research/academic-research/p/paying-too-much-and-being-happy-about-it-existence-causes-and-con); [UCLA PDF](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/marketing/paying_too_much.pdf)). Anxious, aspirational law students match that profile closely. Gym data showing members overpaying on monthly contracts because they overestimated attendance points the same way (DellaVigna & Malmendier, 2006 [K]). Miravete's telephone data cuts the other way: only about 12% chose the flat rate wrongly, which suggests survey studies overstate the bias ([as cited in Lambrecht & Skiera](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/marketing/paying_too_much.pdf)). The honest conclusion is that per-use is the right default, but the case against offering any flat option is weak. A **non-renewing semester pass**, which the earlier report parked as a later experiment, deserves an earlier test.

Guest checkout is the best-evidenced element on the practical side. Baymard's long-running survey ranks forced account creation second among abandonment reasons, after unexpected costs ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/reduce-cart-abandonment)). The figure reported varies by year and secondary source, between roughly 18% and 26%, and **the current figure could not be confirmed on Baymard's own page**. Baymard also reports that 62% of sites still fail to make guest checkout the most prominent option ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/current-state-of-checkout-ux)). These are self-reported reasons, not measured lifts, and no peer-reviewed randomised study of login walls was found. The direction is consistent across years, though, and a deadline-stressed buyer is the worst case for extra steps. The emailed redeem code has no evidence base at all. It is a sensible engineering choice, not a validated one.

## The 10-pack is a compromise anchor, not a decoy

The earlier report justified the 1/3/10 ladder with Ariely's *Economist* example. That example was a hypothetical choice made by about 100 MIT students, a teaching illustration rather than field evidence [K]. More importantly, it involves a **dominated** option: one that is worse on every attribute (Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982 [K]). The 10-pack is cheaper per essay than the 3-pack, so it is an **extreme option**. The relevant mechanism is the compromise effect, in which the middle option gains share when it sits between two extremes (Simonson, 1989 [K]). The best field evidence for that structure is Gu, Kannan and Ma's randomised experiment at the National Academies Press. Adding a higher-priced hardback **raised paperback revenue by 8.9%**, but the same paper warns that a cheap premium option can cannibalise the existing one ([AMA](https://www.ama.org/2018/12/06/winning-the-freemium-to-premium-product-wars/); [UMD](https://www.rhsmith.umd.edu/research/getting-you-pay-freemium-world)). The decoy literature itself is contested: Frederick, Lee and Baskin argue the attraction effect needs restrictive conditions to appear, and Huber and colleagues reply that it is real but narrower than claimed ([replication summary](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/decoy-effect-asymmetric-dominance/); [Duke PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~jch8/bio/Papers/HuberPaynePutoJMR%202014.pdf)). The adjustment is to treat the 10-pack as a product some buyers will choose, such as journal editors, study groups and honours students. Then measure whether it pulls buyers up to the 3-pack or down from A$4.00 to A$3.50 per essay.

The packs themselves are well grounded. Prepayment separates paying from using, so prepaid units are used less, and that unused value is breakage ([MSI](https://www.msi.org/working-papers/transaction-decoupling-the-effects-of-price-bundling-on-the-decision-to-consume/)). Australian gift-card breakage is put at **3–30%, averaging 6–7%** ([SmartCompany](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/retail/what-happens-when-companies-remove-expiry-dates-on-gift-cards/)). Gourville and Soman's own advice is that sellers who want repeat purchase should **encourage early use**, because unused prepaid value lowers renewal ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/3107.html)). "Never expire" goes beyond the three-year gift-card minimum ([Allens](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2018/10/gift-cards-sold-to-australian-consumers-must-be-valid-for-at/)). Whether that regime covers service credits remains unchecked. Keep non-expiry as a reassurance, and nudge pack buyers to use their first credit. There is one counterweight: lab studies found more painful payment methods increased attachment and repeat purchase ([Duke](https://fds.duke.edu/db/aas/pn/faculty/tanya.chartrand/publications/325511)). Frictionless credits may therefore weaken loyalty. For a deadline tool, that trade-off looks acceptable.

## Preview, re-runs and price points rest on thinner ground

The free preview has the right theory behind it. Bawa and Shoemaker's field experiments found free samples produced incremental sales lasting up to about 12 months ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v23y2004i3p345-363.html)). Shampanier, Mazar and Ariely showed a zero price is uniquely powerful: when a Hershey's Kiss became free, its choice share **rose from about 27% to 69%** ([Duke PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf)). That pull is the risk. On a short essay with five errors, a fixed three corrections gives away 60% of the value. **No conversion benchmark was found for locked-results previews.** Freemium SaaS figures of 2–5% describe ongoing free tiers, not a one-off preview at the moment of need ([DBS](https://www.dbs.com/in/sme/businessclass/articles/business-strategy/conversion-rate.page)). The preview also does most of the work of building trust, so one wrong correction among the three shown would cost the sale.

The re-run allowance fits Hart's account of effective guarantees: unconditional, easy to understand, easy to invoke ([HBR](https://store.hbr.org/product/power-of-unconditional-service-guarantees/88405)). It also differentiates Aforecite, because Scribbr charges for rechecks ([Scribbr](https://www.scribbr.com/frequently-asked-questions/annual-subscription-plagiarism-checker/)). Direct evidence is weak, however. One practitioner test found a visible guarantee **raised sales 21% and net revenue about 6.5%**, while another reported a drop in sales when a trusted brand added one ([Quick Sprout](https://www.quicksprout.com/what-converts-better-free-trial-versus-money-back-guarantee/); [Conversion.com](https://conversion.com/?p=8751)). Neither test is peer-reviewed. The adjustment is to present re-runs as part of the price ("edit and re-check free for 14 days") rather than as protection against failure. Keep the earlier cap of three re-runs, and fingerprint footnotes so that a different essay cannot ride a re-run.

On price points, **A$9 is well supported**. A 9-ending reads as cheaper when the leftmost digit changes ([Thomas & Morwitz](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i1p54-64.html)), and catalogue field tests found 9-endings raised demand in all three experiments ([Anderson & Simester](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/qmktec/v1y2003i1p93-110.html)). **A$5 has no specific support.** It is a round price, and no study addresses A$5 against A$7. Round prices may signal quality for anxious, quality-driven purchases (Wadhwa & Zhang, 2015 [K]). Current users have also seen A$4 as a reference price.

## Experiments, not papers, will confirm the model

Each test below should be randomised by visitor and run across a full assessment cycle. Demand peaks at deadlines, so a test that spans a peak and a quiet period will be confounded. Traffic will be small, so expect to detect only large effects. **First, test A$5 against A$7**, with packs scaled to match. Judge it on revenue per preview viewer, not conversion rate; A$7 is wrong if it lowers that figure. **Second, show the line-up with and without the 10-pack.** If the 3-pack's share does not fall when the 10-pack is removed, the anchor is not working; if revenue rises without it, it was cannibalising. **Third, add a non-renewing semester pass.** If it takes more than about 15–20% of buyers and raises revenue per buyer, the per-use-only premise fails. **Fourth, vary preview depth:** three corrections, about 10% of corrections, or the issue count only, each split by footnote count. **Fifth, show or hide re-run messaging at checkout** to price the risk reducer. Instrument from day one: the preview-to-paid funnel, pack mix and revenue per essay, credit redemption and days to redeem, re-run and abuse rates, repeat purchase within the semester, and "lost code" or "wrong correction" support tickets.

Before quoting any number in this report externally, check it against the source. That applies to the [S] figures (Gu et al.'s 8.9%, Shampanier's 27% to 69%, the Baymard abandonment share, the Quick Sprout 21%) and especially to every [K] claim: DellaVigna and Malmendier's gym overpayment, Ariely's *Economist* shares, and the dominance and compromise-effect definitions as stated here.

## Conclusion

The literature changes what the owner should be testing, not whether to launch. The model's real bet is that anxious students want per-essay control. The best field evidence says anxious buyers often want the opposite: a fixed price that stops the meter. The 10-pack and the semester pass are the two places that bet gets settled. "Confirm the model works" therefore means running the A$5/A$7, 10-pack and semester-pass tests through one full assessment cycle. The papers say the design is defensible; only those numbers will say it is right.
