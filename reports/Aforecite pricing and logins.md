# Charge per essay, and keep logins optional

The best way to price Aforecite is **per document, a little above today's price, with a small pack and free re-runs of the same essay**. Concretely: **A$5 per essay (up to 150 footnotes), A$9 for long work (151–400 footnotes), a 3-essay pack at A$12, and a 10-pack at A$35** shown mainly as an anchor. Every purchase should include **up to 3 free re-runs of the same essay for 14 days**. The owner's "about A$4 for ~20 uses" idea is **not a good one**. A typical law student produces roughly 4–10 footnoted documents a year, so 20 uses would last most of a degree. It would cut revenue per paying student by about **75–95%**, and it tells students a high-stakes job is worth A$0.20. The real problem behind the idea is students' fear of paying again after redrafting. Free re-runs fix that directly. On logins: **do not add a login wall**. Single-essay purchases should stay anonymous. When packs launch, hold the balance with an **emailed redeem code**. Add **passwordless email sign-in (6-digit code)** only if packs sell well enough that students need to find their balance across devices. Never build passwords or university Microsoft sign-in. A caveat on sources: every research subagent found direct page fetches blocked by the session's network allowlist. All external figures below therefore come from search-result extracts of the cited pages, not from reading the pages themselves. The final section lists the prices and legal points to check before anything is published.

## "A$4 for 20 uses" would cut revenue per student by 75–95%

The case against the bundle rests on how often students actually need the tool. No national figure exists, but sampled 2026 unit outlines show the usual pattern: one or two AGLC-footnoted pieces per law unit. Examples include a **3,000-word research essay worth 40%** in Sydney's LAWS3431 ([Sydney](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS3431/2026-S1C-PP-CC)), a **1,500-word essay worth 40%** in LAWS5023 ([Sydney](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS5023/2026-S2C-NE-CC)), two 50% essays in a Melbourne jurisprudence subject ([Melbourne](https://handbook.unimelb.edu.au/subjects/laws50127/assessment)), and a **12,000-word honours thesis** in AGLC4 ([Sydney LAWS3900](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS3900/2026-S2C-ND-CC)). With a standard four-unit load, that comes to an estimated **2–5 documents per semester, or 4–10 a year**. Allowing for re-runs after late edits gives about 5–15 runs a year. These are estimates built from outlines, not measured data. Even so, the implication is plain: **20 uses lasts a typical student 1.5–4 years**. The bundle is effectively one A$4 purchase per degree.

The unit economics follow from that. One A$4 sale nets about **A$3.63 on Stripe** (1.65% + A$0.30) or **A$3.91 on Square** (flat 2.2%) ([Stripe support](https://support.stripe.com/questions/updates-to-card-pricing-in-australia-from-october-2026); [Square AU fees](https://www.square.com/au/en/payments/our-fees)). Take a student who runs six essays in a semester. Under per-document pricing they pay six times and net Aforecite about **A$21.65** on Stripe. Under the bundle they pay once and net about **A$3.48**, an 84% fall. At four documents the fall is 75%; at ten it is 90%. To break even, the bundle would need roughly six times as many paying students. The pool of AGLC users is fixed: CALD counted about **36,500 law students in 2015** ([CALD](https://cald.asn.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Prof-Michael-Adams-CALD-Orientation-10-March-2017.pdf)), and graduate counts of about 7,500–8,500 a year suggest roughly 40,000 today ([CALD factsheet](https://cald.asn.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Updated-Factsheet-Law_Students_in_Australia-20-04-2019.pdf); [Pursuit](https://pursuit.unimelb.edu.au/articles/how-australia-got-so-many-law-schools)). As a rough estimate, 2–5% paying penetration is worth about **A$15k–80k a year at A$4 per document**, against **A$3k–8k under the bundle**.

The strongest argument for the idea deserves a fair hearing. Prepaid bundles feel good to buyers: Prelec and Loewenstein's "double-entry" mental accounting shows prepaid use is enjoyed "as if free" ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v17y1998i1p4-28.html)). Consumers also show a reliable flat-rate bias, often prepaying for more than they use ([Lambrecht & Skiera](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/marketing/paying_too_much.pdf)). And a near-free price would certainly lift conversion. But flat-rate bias argues for a pack sized **near** expected use, such as three essays. It does not argue for twenty uses at a token price. The bundle gives away the redraft problem and the volume problem in one move. Only the first needs solving. Today `MAX_RERUNS = 2` covers only footnotes the model failed to read. A student who edits the essay and re-uploads it creates a new session and pays again. A 14-day re-run allowance for the same essay removes that fear while keeping per-essay revenue. Each re-run costs about A$0.03 in compute.

## Students are buying risk reduction, so price above compute but below people

Cost barely constrains the decision. At the most-quoted OpenRouter price for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (**US$0.15 in / US$0.60 out per million tokens**), a typical 100-footnote essay costs about **A$0.03**. Even a worst case where every batch hits the 16k output cap costs about A$0.11 ([pricepertoken](https://pricepertoken.com/pricing-page/model/deepseek-deepseek-v4.1-flash); [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/deepseek/deepseek-v4.1-flash)). Those figures include OpenRouter's **5.5% card top-up fee** ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/simplifying-our-platform-fee/)). **Payment fees, not inference, are the largest variable cost.** Stripe's fixed 30c takes 9.2% of a A$4 sale but only 7.7% of A$5 and 4.1% of A$12. That alone favours fewer, slightly larger transactions. Card surcharging has been banned since **1 October 2026**, so fees cannot be passed on and must be absorbed in the price ([Stripe support](https://support.stripe.com/questions/australia-is-removing-card-surcharges-what-stripe-users-need-to-know)). Free previews run the full pipeline, so each costs the same as a paid document. At a 5% preview-to-paid conversion that adds about A$0.49 per sale, which is affordable while the existing cookie, IP-hash and daily caps hold.

What students will pay depends on what they compare Aforecite against. On one side are paid human services. Scribbr charges **US$2.75 per source** for human citation editing, about A$4, or roughly A$200 for a 50-footnote essay. Its plagiarism check is sold **per document at US$19.95–39.95**, with no subscription ([Scribbr rates](https://www.scribbr.co.uk/proofreading-editing/rates/); [Scribbr](https://app.scribbr.com/plagiarism-checker)). Australian freelance editors charge about **A$26–55 per 1,000 words** ([SUPRA](https://supra.net.au/hdr-hub/proofreaders-and-editors-list/)). Subscription writing tools sit around A$17–43 a month: Grammarly Pro is US$12/month billed annually ([Grammarly](https://www.grammarly.com/pro)) and ChatGPT Plus is A$29.99/month ([aivy](https://aivy.com.au/resources/chatgpt-plans-australia/)). Students already pay small sums for citation help. CiteMe Pro is **US$14.99 a semester** ([CiteMe](https://citeme.app/aglc-citation-generator)), and Scribbr's AI Proofreader is a **US$9.95 30-day pass** ([Scribbr help](https://help.scribbr.com/en/articles/96462-how-much-does-the-ai-proofreader-cost)).

On the other side is free software. **Every AGLC-specific tool found is free.** The closest is Obiter, a GPLv3 Word add-in claiming full AGLC4 support with ibid handling, bibliography generation, document validation and a "Scan and Repair" function ([Obiter](https://obiter.com.au/); [Microsoft Marketplace](https://marketplace.microsoft.com/en-au/product/office/WA200010629)). Others include UNE's SmartCite ([UNE](https://www.une.edu.au/library/students/referencing-and-plagiarism/aglc4-smartcite)), Zotero's ageing AGLC4 style ([Zotero forums](https://forums.zotero.org/discussion/comment/486661/)), and free ChatGPT Edu now rolling out at UNSW and Sydney ([IT Brief](https://itbrief.com.au/story/openai-unsw-sydney-launch-chatgpt-edu-for-80-000); [Sydney](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2026/09/14/students-staff-to-receive-free-chatgpt-edu-access.html)). Money is tight: **58% of students worry about their finances** ([On The Record](https://ontherecordunisa.com/2025/09/10/study-work-worry-repeat-financial-hardship-is-consuming-university-students/)), and studies of US students find most AI users stick to free tiers, with only **11.4%** paying for any AI service ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.00717)).

Taken together, the evidence puts the right price per essay at **roughly A$5–9**. That is well above "trivial" and far below a human editor. Aforecite should not compete with free tools on "AGLC formatting". It should compete on what they do not do: repairing an already-written essay in one pass, renumbering Ibid and "(n x)" cross-references, and returning tracked changes. Universities are also making room for this kind of tool. Sydney permits AI in every "open" assessment ([Sydney](https://www.sydney.edu.au/students/academic-integrity/artificial-intelligence.html)), and UNSW has a category for "standard editing and referencing functions" ([UNSW](https://education.unsw.edu.au/node/780)). That makes "formats your citations, writes nothing, shows every change" a credible message.

## The recommended price ladder

| Product | Price | Net on Stripe / Square | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Essay (≤150 footnotes) | **A$5** | A$4.62 / A$4.89 | Impulse entry point, replaces A$4 |
| Long work (151–400 footnotes) | **A$9** | A$8.55 / A$8.80 | Honours theses, research papers; protects margin on big jobs |
| 3-essay pack | **A$12** (A$4 each) | A$11.50 / A$11.74 | The "sensible" middle option most repeat buyers should pick |
| 10-essay pack | **A$35** | A$34.12 / A$34.23 | Anchor that makes the 3-pack look right; suits journal editors and heavy users |
| Every purchase | up to 3 re-runs of the same essay within 14 days | ~A$0.03 cost each | Removes the fear of paying again after edits |

Each part of the ladder does a job. Size tiering follows Scribbr's length-banded per-document model and charges more for the 12,000-word theses that cost more to process. The 1/3/10 ladder borrows the decoy logic of Ariely's *Economist* experiment, where adding a dominated option moved most buyers to the bundle ([Wikipedia: Decoy effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decoy_effect)). A$5 rather than A$4 leaves room for **GST (1/11 of gross)** if turnover ever passes A$75k, without a later price rise. Students on six essays a semester would net Aforecite about **A$22** under this ladder, against A$3.50 under the bundle. Charm endings have field support: Anderson and Simester found a $9 ending raised demand in all three of their experiments ([Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2003/effects-of-9-price-endings-on-retail-sales-evidence)). Before locking in A$5, the single-essay price is worth A/B testing on the preview page, A$5 against A$7.

Australian law shapes how packs and passes must be designed. **Credits should never expire, or should last at least three years.** Whether a digital credit balance is caught by the gift-card regime's three-year minimum is unsettled ([consumer.gov.au](https://consumer.gov.au/node/161)), and breakage on packs this small is worth little. **Do not auto-renew anything.** The ACCC's 2026–27 priorities target subscription traps ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en-au/knowledge/publications/e88ea652/accc-confirms-2026-27-compliance-and-enforcement-priorities)), and proposed unfair-trading-practice rules would add subscription duties from **1 July 2027** ([Allens](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2026/02/unfair-trading-practices-treasury-unveils-blueprint-for-new-regime/)). **Never write "no refunds".** Consumer guarantees for services cannot be excluded ([ACCC](https://www.accc.gov.au/business/selling-products-and-services/small-business-toolkit/consumer-rights-and-guarantees/introduction)), and unfair contract terms have attracted penalties since November 2023 ([ACCC](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/businesses-urged-to-remove-unfair-contract-terms-ahead-of-law-changes)). Fast self-serve refunds are also the cheaper option: one Stripe dispute costs **A$25, plus A$25 if contested** ([Stripe support](https://support.stripe.com/questions/june-2025-pricing-updates-for-disputes)), which wipes out the margin on about seven to fourteen A$4 sales. Packs should be defined in essays, not dollars. Once Aforecite is GST-registered, document-count packs are taxed simply at sale, while dollar wallets bring voucher-redemption accounting ([ATO](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/gst-excise-and-indirect-taxes/gst/in-detail/rules-for-specific-transactions/gst-and-vouchers)).

Two options are deliberately left out for now. A **monthly subscription** fits lumpy, deadline-driven use badly and carries the most regulatory risk. A **non-renewing semester pass** (about A$19 until a fixed date) is a reasonable later experiment for heavy users. It is not a first build, because it needs persistent identity and overlaps the 10-pack. On processors, Square nets more at every price below about **A$54.55**: about A$0.28 more per A$5 sale, and it may include chargebacks in its 2.2% ([Square developer](https://developer.squareup.com/docs/payments-pricing)). That makes Square the better default rail, provided its checkout can carry the redeem-code flow described below.

## Logins: no wall, a code first, passwordless email only if packs need it

The conversion evidence against forced accounts is strong. In Baymard's survey, **18% of US shoppers abandoned an order because the site wanted them to create an account** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/reduce-cart-abandonment)). Strict password rules cause up to **19% abandonment even among existing account holders** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/current-state-of-checkout-ux)). In Jared Spool's "$300 million button" case, replacing "Register" with "Continue" reportedly added US$300m in a year. The same study found **45% of repeat customers held multiple accounts** because they had forgotten their logins ([Center Centre](https://articles.centercentre.com/three_hund_million_button/); [Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2011/08/05/300-million-button-making-customers-create-logins-to-buy-cost-etailer-300myear.html)). These are e-commerce figures, not Aforecite's. The penalty is plausibly larger for a stressed student buying a A$5 fix the night before a deadline. Aforecite's current flow (anonymous preview, then guest checkout, then download) already matches best practice. Comparable single-task tools such as iLovePDF, Smallpdf and remove.bg let people try without an account and tie only balances to identity ([creativetoolsai](https://www.creativetoolsai.com/free-ai-tools-without-signup/)).

Accounts would earn their keep only once there is something to hold, which means pack credits and the 14-day re-run allowance. They also give a lawful footing for marketing email. Inferred consent under the Spam Act "should not be inferred from one-off purchases" and usually rests on an ongoing relationship such as an account ([Addisons](https://addisons.com/article/the-acma-has-issued-a-statement-about-its-expectations-for-using-consent-to-conduct-e-marketing-and-telemarketing/)). Even then, the lightest identity that works is the right one.

Start with an **emailed redeem code**, about a day's work. On the paid webhook, generate a random code, store only its hash and remaining uses in SQLite, and show it on the success page. Email it to the address the checkout already collected, and add a "resend my codes" form. Move to **passwordless email sign-in** only if pack buyers need a balance across devices. That means a 6-digit one-time code with a 10-minute, single-use expiry and capped attempts, sent through a free tier such as Resend's 3,000 emails a month ([Resend](https://resend.com/blog/new-free-tier)), with a signed session cookie. A typed code is better than a magic link because university mail scanners can consume single-use links. Google sign-in is a few hours' extra work. **Avoid "Sign in with Microsoft" for university accounts.** Microsoft's own education-tenant guidance tells admins to disable user consent, so students would hit "Need admin approval" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/education/guide/1-reference/design-tenant-configurations); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity-platform/consent-types-developer)). Hosted providers like Clerk or WorkOS offer free tiers far beyond Aforecite's scale ([devshot](https://devshot.co/blog/authentication-pricing-compared)). For one solo-run FastAPI app, though, they add a third party holding student identities for little gain.

Logins would not solve preview abuse, and they bring legal duties. Disposable and alias emails make email-verified logins cheap to get around. Requiring a login before the preview would turn the preview into a sign-up wall. Better controls, in rising order of friction, are a bot challenge on upload, a fingerprint of the normalised footnote text (so the same essay cannot draw fresh previews under new cookies), and verified email for the preview only if abuse becomes material. On the legal side, a sole trader under A$3m turnover is generally exempt from the Privacy Act ([OAIC](https://oaic.gov.au/agencies-and-organisations/faqs-for-agencies-orgs/businesses/small-business)). The August 2026 tranche-2 exposure draft leaves that exemption in place ([Clayton Utz](https://www.claytonutz.com/insights/2026/august/the-next-wave-of-australian-privacy-reform-key-proposals-in-the-draft-personal-data-protection-bill-2026)). But the Spam Act applies regardless of size, and the statutory privacy tort has been in force since **10 June 2025** ([NatLawReview](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-serious-invasion-privacy-tort-australia-privacy-comes-effect)). Removing the exemption is agreed government policy in principle. Building to the Australian Privacy Principles now is therefore cheap insurance. In practice: store only email, balance, a credit ledger with payment IDs and a marketing opt-in flag; keep the 24-hour essay deletion; store no passwords; provide self-serve deletion.

## Build order, and what to verify before publishing

The build sequence follows from the above. **First**, change `PRODUCTS` in `aglc/billing.py` to the A$5 essay and A$9 long-work tiers, chosen by the footnote count the preview already knows. Show the tier on the preview page. **Second**, add the 14-day same-essay re-run allowance, issued as a token in the download link. Because essays are deleted after 24 hours, match a re-upload by storing hashed footnote fingerprints, not text, and accepting a re-run when most of them overlap. Replace "the session id is the entitlement" with an entitlement record that the token can reference. **Third**, add the 3-pack and 10-pack, using the emailed redeem code and a credits ledger keyed to the code hash, with webhook credits applied idempotently. Publish terms that state what counts as a use, that credits do not expire, and how refunds work. **Only then**, if pack buyers ask for it, add passwordless email sign-in. Alongside the build, run two cheap validations. One is a short Van Westendorp price survey of 30–50 law students, the approach Patrick Campbell's Price Intelligently popularised ([Antoine Buteau](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-patrick-campbell/)). The other is an A$5 versus A$7 test on the preview page. The owner should also test Obiter's "Scan and Repair" on a messy real essay, because it is the one free tool that could overlap Aforecite's core job.

## Conclusion

The owner's instinct to make Aforecite feel cheap and forgiving is right, but A$0.20 per use is the wrong way to do it. What students fear is paying twice for one essay, not the per-essay price. Free re-runs answer that fear at almost no cost, and the bundle sacrifices most of the revenue to answer the same fear. Inference is about 1% of price, so the price should be set by the value of a cleanly referenced essay and the friction of checkout, not by compute. That points to slightly fewer, slightly larger payments rather than cheaper ones.

The login question mostly answers itself once pricing is settled. Anonymous single purchases are a competitive advantage most comparable tools give up. Identity becomes necessary only when Aforecite starts holding something for the student, such as credits or a re-run window. The lightest identity that holds a balance, a code first and then a passwordless email, keeps the conversion benefit while leaving room for the higher-value segments that will want more: honours students, HDR candidates and student law-journal editors.
