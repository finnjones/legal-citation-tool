# Aforecite pricing and checkout model: what the evidence says

Compiled 1 November 2026. Scope: academic and practitioner evidence on each element of the proposed model (per-use A$5/A$9; 3-pack A$12 and 10-pack A$35 with non-expiring credits; 14-day free re-runs; free 3-correction preview; guest checkout with emailed redeem code; price endings and an A$5 vs A$7 test).

**How to read the source tags.** No full article pages were fetched this session. Every finding comes from one of two places:
- **[S]**: a web-search extract, meaning an abstract, a publisher or university listing, or a secondary summary. The linked URL is the page the extract came from.
- **[K]**: my own knowledge of the paper, not checked against the text this session. Treat [K] effect sizes as approximate and check them before quoting externally.

Where I am unsure what a paper says, I say so. **Lab** means a hypothetical or student-sample experiment; **field** means real purchases.

## 1. Pay-per-use per essay instead of a subscription

**Verdict: Mixed.** Pay-per-use is the right default for infrequent, deadline-driven use. However, the strongest field literature finds that consumers systematically *over*-pay for flat rates and are happy doing so. Leaving out any flat-rate option may therefore give up revenue from anxious students.

**Evidence for**
- Pay-per-use suits buyers whose volume is uncertain or low, and a sticker price per job is easy to judge. remove.bg runs a parallel pay-as-you-go (PAYG) option explicitly for users with uncertain volume who avoid monthly billing. Its PAYG credits last 2 years; subscription credits die when the subscription ends [S] ([remove.bg help](https://www.remove.bg/help/a/what-is-the-difference-between-the-free-plan-subscription-and-pay-as-you-go)).
- Scribbr is the closest student-facing comparison. Its plagiarism checker is sold strictly per document in word-count tiers (US$19.95 / 29.95 / 39.95; UK £13.95 / 22.95 / 31.95), and Scribbr says it does not offer a subscription [S] ([Scribbr](https://www.scribbr.com/frequently-asked-questions/annual-subscription-plagiarism-checker/)). A large, mature academic-tools business has chosen per-document, length-tiered pricing, which is the same structure as A$5/A$9.
- Lambrecht & Skiera (2006, *JMR* 43(2): 212–223) found a pay-per-use bias, meaning customers choose per-use when a flat rate would be cheaper, and that it is driven by *underestimating* usage [S] ([LBS listing](https://www.london.edu/faculty-and-research/academic-research/p/paying-too-much-and-being-happy-about-it-existence-causes-and-con)). For a tool used two to six times a year, underestimation risk is small and per-use is usually the cheaper and more honest option.
- Miravete (2002), using real telephone data, found that only about 12% of customers wrongly chose the flat rate. He argues that survey-based flat-rate-bias findings (Nunes 2000; Prelec & Loewenstein 1998) overstate the bias [S] (as cited in [Lambrecht & Skiera PDF](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/marketing/paying_too_much.pdf)).

**Evidence against**
- **Flat-rate bias is the stronger and more durable effect.** Lambrecht & Skiera (field data from an internet service provider) found the flat-rate bias "more important" and more persistent over time than the pay-per-use bias. They traced it to three causes: an *insurance effect* (avoiding bill variance), a *taxi-meter effect* (disliking the meter running) and *overestimating* usage. Crucially, the flat-rate bias did **not** raise churn, whereas the pay-per-use bias **did** raise churn and wiped out its short-term profit [S] ([LBS](https://www.london.edu/faculty-and-research/academic-research/p/paying-too-much-and-being-happy-about-it-existence-causes-and-con); [Deakin record](https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Paying_too_much_and_being_happy_about_it_existence_causes_and_consequences_of_tariff-choice_biases/20844079)). Anxious students fit the insurance and taxi-meter profile closely.
- Train, McFadden & Ben-Akiva (1987, *RAND J. Econ.*) modelled local telephone tariff choice and found many households chose flat-rate service even where measured service would have been cheaper [K]. Nunes (2000, *J. Behavioral Decision Making*) is cited as survey evidence that consumers who would save on per-use still prefer flat fees [S] (as cited in the [Lambrecht & Skiera PDF](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/marketing/paying_too_much.pdf)). I could not retrieve Nunes's model of how people estimate usage.
- DellaVigna & Malmendier (2006, *AER*), gym field data: monthly-contract members paid roughly US$17 per visit when a US$10 per-visit pass existed, because they overestimated how often they would attend [K]. This is overestimation in a goal-driven, aspirational setting, which resembles a student planning to "use it for every essay".
- Ascarza, Lambrecht & Vilcassim (2012, *JMR*, "When talk is 'free'") found that including free units in a tariff changes usage beyond the price effect [S] (title only, from [LBS](https://london.edu/faculty-and-research/academic-research/w/when-talk-is-free-the-effect-of-tariff-structure-on-usage-under-two-and-three-part-tariffs-p9446)). Iyengar and co-authors' work on three-part tariffs and retention (*Marketing Science*, 2011) is relevant, but I did not verify its findings this session [K].

**Conditions.** Per-use wins when volume is low, predictable to the buyer and lumpy. Flat rate wins when buyers fear variance or overestimate their usage. Law students meet both conditions at once: usage is low, but they are anxious and aspirational.

**Design implications.** Keep per-use as the default. Consider testing a time-boxed "semester pass" (for example, unlimited essays to a set date, one-off payment, no auto-renew) as a third option. It would capture the insurance and taxi-meter willingness to pay without the cost of policing a subscription. The prepaid packs already partly play this role.

## 2. Prepaid bundles (3 for A$12, 10 for A$35), credits never expire, 10-pack as anchor or decoy

**Verdict: Mixed. The bundle idea is Supported, but the "decoy" mechanism is mislabelled and Weak on real-purchase evidence.**

**Evidence for: prepayment and decoupling**
- Prelec & Loewenstein (1998, *Marketing Science*, "The red and the black") propose prospective accounting: paying in advance makes later consumption feel free, and people prefer to prepay for consumption goods. The flat-rate bias follows from the same model [K].
- Soman & Gourville (2001, *JMR*, "Transaction decoupling") and Gourville & Soman (2002, *HBR*, "Pricing and the psychology of consumption") found that bundling hides the link between cost and benefit, so people use prepaid service bundles *less*. Theatre and ski-pass holders were less likely to use the last units [S] ([MSI working paper](https://www.msi.org/working-papers/transaction-decoupling-the-effects-of-price-bundling-on-the-decision-to-consume/); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/3107.html)). For revenue, this means bundles create breakage. Gourville & Soman (1998, *JCR*), gym data: attendance peaked just after each payment and decayed as the payment receded ("payment depreciation") [K].
- **Payment friction.** Each card transaction on A$5 carries a fixed processor fee. Stripe Australia is roughly 1.7% + A$0.30 for domestic cards [K, verify], so about 7–8% of a A$5 sale goes to fees, against about 4% on A$12. Fewer, larger transactions also reduce repeated pain of paying (see section 7).
- Breakage. Australian Treasury's consumer affairs council cited industry gift-card breakage of 3–30%, with analysts putting the average at 6–7% [S] ([SmartCompany/The Conversation](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/retail/what-happens-when-companies-remove-expiry-dates-on-gift-cards/); [Treasury report](https://treasury.gov.au/publication/gift-cards-in-the-australian-market-report-2/gift-cards-in-the-australian-market-report/part-ii-the-australian-gift-card-market)).

**Evidence against or caveats**
- Gourville & Soman's own advice is that sellers who want *renewal* should encourage early use, because unused prepaid value lowers repurchase [S] ([HBS WK](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/3107.html)). Breakage is short-term revenue but can weaken habit and word of mouth.
- **"Never expire" is legally generous, not necessary.** Australian gift-card rules from 1 November 2019 require at least 3 years' validity [S] ([Allens](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2018/10/gift-cards-sold-to-australian-consumers-must-be-valid-for-at/)). Whether those rules cover service credits is a legal question I have not checked. Non-expiry also creates an open-ended deferred-revenue liability.
- Stremersch & Tellis (2002, *JM*) is a framework for bundling strategy, not an effect-size study. It separates price bundling from product bundling and pure from mixed bundling [K]. Offering singles alongside packs is *mixed* bundling, which the framework generally treats as more robust than packs alone.

**The "decoy" problem**
- A classic asymmetric-dominance decoy (Huber, Payne & Puto 1982, *JCR*) must be clearly *worse* than the target on every attribute [K]. **The 10-pack is not dominated by the 3-pack:** it costs A$3.50 per essay against A$4.00. It is not a decoy. It is an *extreme option*, so the relevant mechanism is the **compromise effect** (Simonson 1989, *JCR*), in which the middle option gains share when it sits between two extremes [K]. Single, 3-pack and 10-pack form exactly that structure.
- **Best field evidence for this structure:** Gu, Kannan & Ma (2018, *JM*, Paul Root Award) ran a randomised field experiment at the National Academies Press. Adding a higher-priced hardback raised *paperback* revenue by 8.9%, attributed to a compromise effect, and individual choice data also showed attraction effects. **Caveat from the same paper:** a cheaper premium option can cannibalise the existing premium product [S] ([AMA summary](https://www.ama.org/2018/12/06/winning-the-freemium-to-premium-product-wars/); [UMD](https://www.rhsmith.umd.edu/research/getting-you-pay-freemium-world)).
- **Replication debate.** Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014, *JMR* 51(4): 487–507, "The limits of attraction") and Yang & Lynn (2014) argue that the attraction effect needs such restrictive conditions (stylised numeric stimuli, clearly dominated options) that its practical value is doubtful [S] ([replication summary](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/decoy-effect-asymmetric-dominance/); [EUR thesis review](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/18725/MA-thesis-Roks-T.-360676-.pdf)). As I recall, they report that the effect largely vanished with pictorial or realistic stimuli and sometimes reversed [K]. Huber, Payne & Puto replied in the same issue (2014) that the effect is real but narrower than commonly claimed [S] ([Duke PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~jch8/bio/Papers/HuberPaynePutoJMR%202014.pdf)). Other replications are mixed; for example, a Czech study found the effect in only 2 of 4 categories [S] ([MUNI](https://muni.cz/en/research/publications/1159479)).
- Ariely's *Economist* example (*Predictably Irrational*): web-only US$59, print-only US$125, print+web US$125. With the dominated print-only option present, 84% chose print+web; without it, 32% did [K]. This was a hypothetical choice by about 100 MIT students. It is a teaching example, not field evidence.

**Design implications.** Describe the 10-pack internally as a compromise-effect anchor. Make it a *genuinely* sensible buy, for example for study groups and multi-essay semesters, because some buyers will choose it and it should not feel like a trick. Decide how a long document (A$9) uses credits; for instance, one long document could cost 2 credits, which would make the packs consistent. Consider a 3-year validity in place of "never" only if the liability matters; "never expire" is a strong anxiety reducer and good marketing.

## 3. Free re-runs of the same essay for 14 days

**Verdict: Supported in principle (risk-reducer theory). Weak direct evidence on the exact mechanic.**

**Evidence for**
- Hart (1988, *HBR*, "The power of unconditional service guarantees") says an effective guarantee is unconditional, easy to understand, meaningful, easy to invoke and quick to pay out [S] ([HBR store](https://store.hbr.org/product/power-of-unconditional-service-guarantees/88405); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_guarantee)). An automatic re-run with no claim form meets all of these.
- It directly neutralises the *taxi-meter* and *insurance* motives found by Lambrecht & Skiera (section 1) [S]. The fear being removed ("will I pay again after edits?") is exactly a variance fear.
- It differentiates Aforecite from its closest analogue. Scribbr charges full price for plagiarism rechecks [S] ([Scribbr](https://www.scribbr.com/frequently-asked-questions/annual-subscription-plagiarism-checker/)).
- Andreoni and colleagues found in a laboratory trust experiment that sellers who offered satisfaction guarantees behaved more trustworthily than those who did not; guarantees work partly as signals [S] ([UCSD working paper](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreoni/WorkingPapers/Satisfaction20171028.pdf)) (lab).

**Evidence against or caveats**
- Practitioner tests go both ways. One A/B test found a visible 30-day money-back guarantee raised sales 21%, with 12% refunds and about +6.5% net revenue. Another reported a double-digit *drop* in sales when a guarantee was added for a trusted brand, because it introduced doubt [S] ([Quick Sprout](https://www.quicksprout.com/what-converts-better-free-trial-versus-money-back-guarantee/); [Conversion.com](https://conversion.com/?p=8751)). Neither is peer-reviewed or reports sample sizes.
- Wirtz & Kum (2004, *JAMS* 32(2): 159–175, "Consumer cheating on service guarantees") [S] (citation only, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_guarantee)). As I recall, cheating increases when it is easy and anonymous and falls with repeat-purchase intent; I am not confident about the effect of payout size [K]. **Abuse risk here:** "same essay" must be fingerprinted (for example, by footnote overlap) so that a different essay cannot ride a re-run.
- Heiman, McWilliams & Zilberman (2001, *J. Business Research*) model money-back guarantees and demonstrations as substitute ways of reducing uncertainty [K]. If so, the free preview and the re-run guarantee overlap, so test the marginal value of each.

**Design implications.** Frame the re-run as included in the price ("Edit and re-check free for 14 days"), not as a guarantee that hints at failure. Track re-run abuse. Consider whether a simple refund policy for outright failures adds anything beyond the re-run.

## 4. Free preview: first 3 corrections shown in full, the rest locked

**Verdict: Supported for sampling a product whose quality is unknown, with real cannibalisation risk on short essays.**

**Evidence for**
- Bawa & Shoemaker (2004, *Marketing Science* 23(3)) report two **field** experiments in which free samples produced incremental sales lasting up to about 12 months. Their model includes acceleration and cannibalisation components [S] ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v23y2004i3p345-363.html)); I could not retrieve the magnitudes.
- Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely (2007, *Marketing Science* 26(6)): cutting a price to zero has an outsized effect. When the Kiss became free, choice of the Hershey's Kiss over the Lindt truffle jumped from about 27% to 69% [S] ([Duke PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf)) (lab and field-stand). A free first step lowers the barrier to engagement.
- Gu, Kannan & Ma (2018) is the leading field study on freemium. The design of the premium tier and the line around the free product drive conversion [S] ([AMA](https://www.ama.org/2018/12/06/winning-the-freemium-to-premium-product-wars/)).

**Evidence against or caveats**
- **Zero-price pull cuts both ways:** if the free part solves enough of the problem, people take it and leave. A short essay with 5 errors gets 60% fixed free. The lock should be defined by the share of value delivered, not a fixed count.
- Freemium conversion benchmarks are low: 2–5% is commonly attributed to Kumar (2014, *HBR*, "Making 'freemium' work"), with 2–4% (Tunguz) and a reported median of 8% in one product-led report [S] ([DBS](https://www.dbs.com/in/sme/businessclass/articles/business-strategy/conversion-rate.page); [Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/freemium-to-premium)). These figures are for *ongoing* SaaS free tiers. A one-shot preview at the moment of need is closer to a trial or demonstration and should convert far higher, but **I found no benchmark for "locked-results previews"**.
- A preview exposes quality. If any of the 3 shown corrections is wrong, trust collapses at the worst moment. Heiman et al.'s view that demonstrations substitute for guarantees [K] implies that the preview carries most of the risk-reduction load.

**Design implications.** Show the full error count (for example, "47 issues found; 3 shown") to create a gap between what the student knows and what they want to know. Choose the 3 shown to be the highest-confidence corrections. Measure the preview-to-pay conversion rate split by document size.

## 5. Guest checkout, emailed redeem code, passwordless sign-in later

**Verdict: Supported (practitioner evidence, consistent across years). Weak peer-reviewed evidence.**

**Evidence for**
- Baymard's long-running abandonment survey lists forced account creation as the second most-cited checkout-abandonment reason after unexpected costs. Secondary sources give 19%, 24% or 26% depending on year and sample (US; Baymard's 2025 sample of about 4,560 is cited) [S] ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/reduce-cart-abandonment); [RetailBoss/Statista](https://retailboss.co/10-reasons-for-abandonment-during-checkout-in-the-u-s); [LaunchMyStore](https://launchmystore.io/blog/checkout-optimization-frictionless-ecommerce)). **I could not confirm the exact current percentage on Baymard's own page.** Baymard reports that 62% of sites still fail to make guest checkout the most prominent option [S] ([Baymard current state](https://baymard.com/blog/current-state-of-checkout-ux)).
- The purchase occurs under deadline stress, and every added step costs a share of near-deadline buyers.

**Evidence against or caveats**
- These are self-reported survey reasons, not measured lifts; respondents can name more than one reason. I found **no peer-reviewed randomised study** of registration walls and conversion.
- Redeem codes held only by email risk loss and support load ("I lost my code"), confusion between code and account, and code sharing or leakage. Over time, accounts help retention and re-marketing.

**Design implications.** Keep guest-first. Have the code arrive automatically from the purchase email address (a magic link), and let any later passwordless sign-in with that email adopt the existing balance. Track "lost code" support tickets.

## 6. Price endings (5/9 vs round) and A$5 vs A$7

**Verdict: Mixed. 9-endings have field support at the left-digit boundary. For whole-dollar digital prices the evidence is thin, and a round price can signal quality.**

**Evidence for**
- Anderson & Simester (2003, *QME* 1: 93–110), three **field** experiments in catalogues: $9 endings raised demand in all three; the lift was larger for new products and weaker when "Sale" cues were present [S] ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/qmktec/v1y2003i1p93-110.html); [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2003/effects-of-9-price-endings-on-retail-sales-evidence/)). One reported example is a dress at $39 drawing about a third more demand than at $34, with no gain at $44 [S, unverified against the paper; matches my recollection K].
- Thomas & Morwitz (2005, *JCR* 32(1): 54–64): 9-endings feel cheaper **only when the leftmost digit changes** ($2.99 vs $3.00), and more so when the comparison price is close [S] ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i1p54-64.html); [EurekAlert](https://eurekalert.org/news-releases/545204)) (lab).
- Schindler & Kibarian (1996, *J. Retailing*), mail-order field test: 99-endings increased sales by roughly 8% against 00-endings [K, approximate].

**Evidence against or caveats**
- A$5 itself is a round price; the "ends in 5" idea has no evidence base beyond cognitive fluency. A$9 for long documents captures a left-digit benefit against A$10. A$4.99 would beat A$5.00 on left-digit grounds, but adds cents to a digital checkout.
- Round prices signal quality and fit purchases made on feeling (Stiving 2000, *Management Science*; Wadhwa & Zhang 2015, *JCR*) [K]. Law students' purchases are anxious and driven by quality, which pushes towards round prices.
- **A$5 vs A$7:** no study answers this. Theory says demand is likely inelastic at the moment of need, and A$7 for a high-stakes essay is still trivial against the grade at risk. Current users have seen A$4, a reference-price anchor, so any rise will be judged against it.

**Design implications.** Run A$5 vs A$7 as a randomised test judged on **revenue per preview viewer**, not conversion rate. Keep A$9 for long documents. If A$7 is chosen, re-price the packs so that per-essay savings stay visible.

## 7. Cross-cutting: pain of paying and transaction friction

**Verdict: Supports fewer, larger, prepaid transactions; weakly cuts against single A$5 charges.**
- Zellermayer (1996, Carnegie Mellon dissertation, "The pain of paying") coined the construct [K]. Prelec & Simester (2001, *Marketing Letters*, "Always leave home without it"), with real tickets and sealed bids: credit-card payers bid substantially more, by up to about 100% in one study, than cash payers [K, verify size].
- Shah, Eisenkraft, Bettman & Chartrand (2016, *JCR* 42(5): 688–708): *more* painful payment methods increased later connection to the product and the likelihood of a repeat transaction [S] ([Duke record](https://fds.duke.edu/db/aas/pn/faculty/tanya.chartrand/publications/325511); [U of T](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/paper-or-plastic-u-t-study-finds-how-we-pay-things-may-affect-how-we-feel-about-them)). **This partly counters the low-friction argument:** frictionless prepaid credits may weaken attachment. These were lab studies, and the effect concerns payment method more than transaction count.
- Implication: prepaid packs reduce the pain of paying per essay (decoupling), at the cost of lower usage and attachment (Soman & Gourville). For a tool used at deadlines, that trade-off looks acceptable.

## 8. Comparable tools

- **Scribbr:** per-document, length-tiered pricing, no subscription, paid rechecks [S] ([Scribbr](https://www.scribbr.com/frequently-asked-questions/annual-subscription-plagiarism-checker/)). This is the most comparable market and it validates per-use plus length tiers.
- **remove.bg:** free tier, subscription, and PAYG credits (2-year validity) as a back-up to subscription credits [S] ([remove.bg](https://www.remove.bg/help/a/what-is-the-difference-between-the-free-plan-subscription-and-pay-as-you-go)). A hybrid serves both buyer types.
- **Stock photography (iStock/Shutterstock):** long-standing credit packs alongside subscriptions. iStock credits have historically expired after about 12 months [K, verify].
- **Midjourney, ElevenLabs:** generative tools sold mainly by subscription with credit allowances. Midjourney suspended its free trial in 2023, citing abuse [K]. These are frequent-use creative tools, so they are weak analogues.
- **Gap:** I found no published before-and-after data on what happened when any of these firms changed pricing model.

## Overall verdict

**The configuration is coherent and each part has a recognised rationale, but it has not been validated as a package.** The configuration is per-use, plus a small prepaid pack, plus a risk reducer, plus a free preview, with no forced login. The strongest support is for guest checkout (consistent practitioner data), per-use for low-frequency jobs (Scribbr, remove.bg), sampling and preview (Bawa & Shoemaker field data), and a three-option line-up that creates a compromise effect (Gu et al. field experiment). The weakest links are:
- the "decoy" label: the 10-pack is not a dominated decoy, and real-purchase evidence for decoys is contested;
- price-ending claims for whole-dollar digital prices;
- the assumption that consumers prefer per-use: the field literature says anxious consumers prefer, and happily overpay for, flat rates.

I found **no study of this exact bundle** for low-frequency, high-stakes digital purchases. Its elements come from different settings: telephone, gym, catalogue, books and lab chocolate.

## Key risks

1. **Unused flat-rate willingness to pay:** anxious students might pay more for a semester pass (insurance and taxi-meter effects), and per-use alone leaves this on the table.
2. **Preview cannibalisation** on short or clean essays (zero-price pull).
3. **Preview errors** destroying trust at the decision point.
4. **10-pack cannibalising the 3-pack:** if many buyers choose the A$3.50-per-essay pack, average revenue per essay falls (Gu et al.'s cannibalisation caveat).
5. **Re-run abuse** (different essay passed off as the same) and open-ended **credit liability** from never-expiring credits.
6. **Redeem-code loss** and support cost.
7. **Reference-price backlash** moving from A$4 to A$5 or A$7.
8. **Processor fees** eroding A$5 single sales.

## What would falsify it: metrics and experiments

**Metrics (instrument from day one):**
- preview → checkout start → paid, split by footnote count;
- pack mix (single, 3-pack, 10-pack) and average revenue per essay;
- credit redemption rate and days-to-redeem, giving breakage and liability;
- re-run rate per paid essay, and the flagged-abuse rate;
- repeat purchase within the semester;
- refunds and support tickets ("lost code", "wrong correction");
- checkout abandonment at each step.

**Experiments (randomised by visitor):**
1. **A$5 vs A$7** (packs scaled to match), judged on revenue per preview viewer over a full assessment cycle. Falsified if A$7 lowers revenue per viewer.
2. **With vs without the 10-pack.** If 3-pack share does not fall when the 10-pack is removed, the anchor is not working. If revenue rises without it, it was cannibalising.
3. **Add a "semester pass"** (one-off, no auto-renew). If it captures more than about 15–20% of buyers and lifts revenue per buyer, the per-use-only premise is falsified.
4. **Preview depth:** 3 corrections vs about 10% of corrections vs count-only. Find the conversion peak.
5. **Re-run messaging:** shown vs hidden at checkout, to measure the marginal conversion value of the risk reducer.
6. **Price ending:** A$5 vs A$4.95, and A$9 vs A$10, as a low-priority check.

Use full assessment cycles (exam and essay peaks) as test windows. Demand is seasonal, so tests that run across a deadline peak and a quiet period will be confounded.
