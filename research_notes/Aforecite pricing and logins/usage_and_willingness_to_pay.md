# Aforecite: How often students need an AGLC4 footnote fixer, and what they will pay

Research date: 1 November 2026. **Method caveat:** this session's network blocked direct page fetches for almost every relevant domain (cald.asn.au, universitiesaustralia.edu.au, sydney.edu.au, education.gov.au, reddit.com, law.unimelb.edu.au and others all refused). Every finding below comes from web-search result summaries, not from reading the full pages. Figures are cited to the page the search surfaced, but they should be spot-checked against the original before they go into anything public. Older data is flagged inline.

Note on a related source: the search surfaced the public GitHub repo `finnjones/legal-citation-tool` ("AGLC4 legal citation formatter for Word essays"), which appears to be Aforecite's own open-source codebase. That matters for pricing: technically capable students can run it themselves with their own model key.

## 1. How many AGLC-referenced documents does a typical LLB/JD student produce, and would they re-run drafts?

### Takeaway
No source gives a national "essays per semester" figure, and assessment mixes vary a lot by unit. Sampled unit outlines show most law units have one or two AGLC-footnoted written pieces (1,500 to 6,000 words), often alongside an exam. A reasonable estimate for a full-time student is **about 2–5 AGLC-footnoted documents per semester, or about 4–10 per year**, plus a 12,000–15,000-word thesis for honours students. Counting re-runs of drafts, that is roughly **5–15 tool runs per student per year**.

### Cited Findings
- University of Sydney LAWS3431 (S1 2026): the research essay is worth 40%, capped at 3,000 words with footnotes excluded, must follow the current AGLC edition, and needs a 200-word abstract by Week 5 — [Sydney unit outline LAWS3431](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS3431/2026-S1C-PP-CC)
- Sydney LAWS5023 (S2 2026): the independent research essay is capped at 1,500 words and worth 40%. Substantive footnotes count toward the limit, and the outline also says the total word count includes all footnotes and any bibliography — [Sydney unit outline LAWS5023](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS5023/2026-S2C-NE-CC)
- Sydney LAWS6252 (intensive, 2026): the research essay is worth 40%, capped at 2,000 words with citations included, and AGLC4 is expected — [Sydney unit outline LAWS6252](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS6252/2026-S1CIAP-BM-CC)
- Sydney LAWS3900 Law Honours (2026): a 12,000-word thesis, including discursive footnotes, in AGLC4 — [Sydney unit outline LAWS3900](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS3900/2026-S2C-ND-CC)
- Sydney LAWS5165 (2024, a law review unit): a 6,000-word essay worth 80% plus a 1,000-word critical review worth 20% — [Sydney unit outline LAWS5165](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS5165/2024-S2C-ND-CC)
- Macquarie: one unit sets an 8,000-word research essay and another a 15,000-word maximum, footnotes excluded in both — [Macquarie unit guide](https://unitguides.mq.edu.au/unit_offerings/96395/unit_guide); [Macquarie unit guide](https://unitguides.mq.edu.au/unit_offerings/96106/unit_guide)
- Melbourne, Philosophical Foundations of Law: two essays on different topics, each worth 50% — [Melbourne handbook LAWS50127](https://handbook.unimelb.edu.au/subjects/laws50127/assessment)
- Melbourne: one subject (name unclear from the snippet, updated 2025) sets a 5,000–6,000-word take-home exam worth 100% — [Melbourne handbook](https://handbook.unimelb.edu.au/2017/subjects/laws70060/assessment). The JD Legal Research capstone listed an 8,000-word research project worth 100% (**2014 data**) — [Melbourne archived handbook LAWS50039](https://archive.handbook.unimelb.edu.au/view/2014/LAWS50039)
- A Melbourne LAW5000 assignment sheet, hosted on a third-party site so unverified, sets a penalty of 1 mark per 100 words over the limit, and analysis in footnotes counts toward the limit — [CliffsNotes copy of LAW5000 instructions](https://www.cliffsnotes.com/study-notes/4167909)
- Western Sydney LAWS1007 Introduction to Law: a 1,500-word essay worth 30% — [WSU handbook LAWS1007](https://hbook.westernsydney.edu.au/subject-details/laws1007)
- Search summaries of these outlines describe the usual pattern as one or two major essays combined with a test or exam, with the mix varying by unit — [Sydney LAWS5165](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS5165/2024-S2C-ND-CC); [Melbourne LAWS50127](https://handbook.unimelb.edu.au/subjects/laws50127/assessment)
- Sydney's assessment framework: from Semester 2, 2025 every assessment is either "secure" (supervised, AI prohibited) or "open" (unsupervised, AI permitted). The university says secure assessment covers in-person tasks such as oral assessments and written exams — [Sydney news, Nov 2024](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2024/11/27/university-of-sydney-ai-assessment-policy.html); [Sydney news, Sep 2026](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2026/09/14/ai-success-measured-by-what-it-helps-our-people-achieve.html)
- A Deakin Law Students' Society piece is titled "Law student realises they shouldn't have left referencing until the end", which shows the last-minute referencing pain point — [Deakin LSS](https://www.deakinlss.org/post/this-just-in-law-student-realises-they-shouldn-t-have-left-referencing-until-the-end)

### Inferences
- **Documents per semester (estimate).** A full-time load is normally 4 units per semester. *(This is the standard Australian load; I found no source to cite.)* If 50–100% of law units include at least one AGLC-footnoted written piece (essay, research paper, take-home exam, or a problem answer with footnotes), and some units have two (eg Melbourne's two-essay subject), then:
  - low case: 4 × 0.5 × 1 = **2 documents per semester**
  - high case: 4 × 1.0 × 1.25 = **5 documents per semester**
  - **That gives about 4–10 documents per year for a full-time student.** Part-time JD students (2–3 units per semester) would produce about 2–6 a year.
- **Combined degrees.** Many LLB students take combined degrees and only about half their units are law. A combined-degree student in a non-law semester may produce zero AGLC documents, so the per-year average across all LLB students is probably near the low end, about **4–6**.
- **Footnote counts (estimate, no source).** Footnote-heavy legal essays often run about 1 footnote per 30–60 words. On that assumption, a 1,500-word essay has about 25–50 footnotes, a 3,000-word essay about 50–100, and a 12,000-word honours thesis about 200–400. Theses are much bigger jobs per document.
- **Re-runs.** Aforecite returns tracked changes, so a student would plausibly run a near-final draft, accept the changes, then sometimes re-run after late edits that move footnotes. That is because Ibid and "(n x)" cross-references break whenever footnotes are inserted or reordered. A multiplier of about 1.2–2 runs per document is plausible, giving **about 5–15 runs per student per year**. Theses and long research papers are more likely to be re-run several times across chapters and drafts.
- **Fit with "20 uses for A$4".** At about 5–15 runs a year, 20 uses lasts about 1.5–4 years, so for most LLB/JD students it would be a single purchase over the whole degree. Compared with A$4 per document (about A$16–60 a year for a user who pays every time), it cuts revenue per active user by roughly 75–95%.
- **Effect of exam policy.** The move to secure, in-person exams (Sydney's two-lane model) may shift some take-home exams to supervised exams, slightly lowering document counts. Research essays sit in the "open" lane and are unaffected.
- **Word limits.** Units that count footnotes toward the word limit (Sydney LAWS5023 and LAWS6252) make students sensitive to changes in footnote length. Short titles and "(n x)" usually shorten footnotes, which is a selling point.

### Gaps
- No national survey or CALD data on the number or type of written assessments per law unit. Current Melbourne JD handbook entries could not be loaded.
- No source for typical footnote counts per essay. The figures above are estimates.
- No data on how often students revise and re-reference drafts.
- The semester calendar (assessment peaks) could not be fetched from a university source; see section 3.

## 2. Who else uses AGLC4, and which segments are worth more?

### Takeaway
AGLC is the de facto citation standard across Australian law schools, courts, and law journals, and more than 30 journals have formally adopted it. The higher-value segments are honours and HDR thesis writers (one document of 12,000+ words, revised repeatedly) and student law-journal editors, who check AGLC on many articles per issue. AGLC5 was not yet published as of September 2026, so AGLC4 is still current.

### Cited Findings
- AGLC is the most widely used legal citation standard in Australia — [Wikipedia: Australian Guide to Legal Citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Guide_to_Legal_Citation). It is described as the de facto standard across law schools, courts, and many legal publications — [Bond University AGLC guide](https://bond.libguides.com/aglc/home). MULR calls it the authoritative legal citation guide in Australia — [MULR: About AGLC](https://law.unimelb.edu.au/mulr/aglc/about)
- Deakin Law School requires AGLC4 — [Deakin legal referencing guide](https://deakin.libguides.com/legal-referencing/legal-referencing). So do Adelaide Law School ([Adelaide Writing Centre](https://www.adelaide.edu.au/writingcentre/sites/default/files/docs/aglc.pdf)) and Melbourne Law School ([UniMelb library guide](https://unimelb.libguides.com/LAW-Student/referencing)). AGLC4 was published by Melbourne University Press in February 2018 — [Boffins Bookshop listing](https://www.boffinsbookshop.com.au/books/9780646976389/australian-guide-to-legal-citation-4e)
- More than 30 Australian and overseas law journals have adopted AGLC as their official style guide — [MULR Editors' Note](https://law.unimelb.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/5630884/Editors-Note.pdf). The UNSW Law Journal uses AGLC4 plus its own "Additions" — [UNSW Law Journal submissions](https://www.unsw.edu.au/research/unswlawjournal/submission)
- Several journals are run by student editors: MULR and MJIL, whose members are all current Melbourne Law School students ([MJIL about](https://law.unimelb.edu.au/mjil/about); [Wikipedia: MULR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne_University_Law_Review)); the UNSW Law Journal, whose board is entirely voluntary students ([UNSW Law Journal about](https://www.unsw.edu.au/research/unswlawjournal/about-us)); and the Adelaide Law Review, whose student editors take coursework units LAWS3015/3016 ([Adelaide Law Review](https://adelaide.edu.au/about/school/law/our-research/adelaide-law-review/))
- Honours theses: 12,000 words in AGLC4 at Sydney ([LAWS3900](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS3900/2026-S2C-ND-CC)). Research papers at Macquarie run 8,000–15,000 words ([Macquarie unit guide](https://unitguides.mq.edu.au/unit_offerings/96106/unit_guide))
- Monash asks graduate research students about their generative AI use at every progress milestone — [Monash Graduate Research: Guidance on Generative AI](https://www.monash.edu/graduate-research/support-and-resources/guidance-on-generative-ai)
- AGLC5: consultations ran 2023–2024 and are now closed. As of 21 September 2026 there was no published AGLC5 and no release date, so AGLC4 (2018) remains current — [MULR AGLC5 page](https://law.unimelb.edu.au/mulr/aglc/aglc-5); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Guide_to_Legal_Citation); [AGLC tweet calling for AGLC5 feedback, Oct 2023](https://x.com/AGLCTweets/status/1716588392253976993)
- Non-law disciplines: the "law and paralegal studies" field (used in Graduate Outcomes Survey reporting) includes legal studies students alongside LLB/JD students — [LSJ](https://lsj.com.au/articles/is-australia-producing-too-many-law-graduates/). Swinburne, Southern Cross, CDU and UniSQ publish AGLC4 library guides — [Swinburne](https://swinburne.edu.au/library/referencing/aglc4); [SCU](https://libguides.scu.edu.au/aglc/about-aglc); [CDU](https://libguides.cdu.edu.au/CDUAGLC); [UniSQ](https://usq-qld.libguides.com/aglc/about)

### Inferences
- **Value by segment, highest first:**
  1. **HDR/PhD and LLM-by-research students:** 80,000–100,000-word theses with thousands of footnotes (typical Australian law PhD length; not sourced here), revised over years. They will pay much more per document, or would want a thesis tier priced by footnote count.
  2. **Honours students:** a 12,000–15,000-word thesis with about 200–400 footnotes (estimate), high stakes, plus normal coursework.
  3. **Student law-journal editors:** several student-run journals (MULR, MJIL, UNSW LJ, Adelaide LR and others) check AGLC on every article. This is a natural institutional or "editor pass" segment with heavy, repeated use, and plausibly budget-holding. Each issue carries several articles of 10,000+ words.
  4. **Academics and practitioners** writing for AGLC journals: low volume but higher income. Courts use AGLC too, though practitioners mostly cite in court-specific styles.
  5. **Coursework LLB/JD students:** the largest group by volume, but the most price-sensitive.
  6. **Legal studies, criminology and justice students** in units that require AGLC: a long tail.
- **AGLC5 risk and opportunity.** When AGLC5 is published, Aforecite will need updates. The changeover would also create a spike in demand, because every student and editor would have to re-learn the rules.

### Gaps
- No count found for law HDR students (PhD/SJD/LLM research) or honours students nationally.
- No complete list of Australian student-edited law journals or their editorial board sizes.
- No direct data on how widely AGLC is used in criminology and politics.

## 3. Market size and seasonality

### Takeaway
Australia has about 39–40 law schools. The most recent sourced enrolment figure is **36,500 law students in 2015** (CALD), which is old. CALD has put LLB/JD graduates at about 7,500–8,500 a year, while broader counts (including law and legal studies) reach about 15,000. An estimate of **about 35,000–50,000 current LLB+JD students and about 10,000 commencing each year** is reasonable. Assessment peaks are almost certainly in the back half of each semester (roughly April–June and September–November). I could not cite a source for this.

### Cited Findings
- A 2017 CALD presentation reported more than 36,500 law students across 39 law schools in 2015 (**2015 data**) — [CALD, Prof Michael Adams orientation slides, Mar 2017](https://cald.asn.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Prof-Michael-Adams-CALD-Orientation-10-March-2017.pdf); see also the [CALD factsheet "Law Students in Australia"](https://cald.asn.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Factsheet-Law_Students_in_Australia-1.pdf), which could not be opened
- A CALD survey counted 8,499 LLB and JD graduates starting employment or entering the job market in 2018 (**2018 data**) — [CALD factsheet, updated 2019](https://cald.asn.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Updated-Factsheet-Law_Students_in_Australia-20-04-2019.pdf)
- CALD says about 7,500 law students graduate each year, while some put the figure at 15,000 — [Pursuit (UniMelb), "How Australia got so many law schools"](https://pursuit.unimelb.edu.au/articles/how-australia-got-so-many-law-schools)
- More than 15,000 law graduates a year from 40 law schools — [Law Society Journal, "Is Australia producing too many law graduates?"](https://lsj.com.au/articles/is-australia-producing-too-many-law-graduates/). A 2025 UQ Law Journal paper puts graduates at about 15,000 in 2025 and counts 39 universities offering primary law degrees — [UQLJ 2025](https://journal.law.uq.edu.au/index.php/uqlj/article/download/17243/12342/79946)
- The higher counts tend to merge law with legal studies, and combined-degree students are often recorded under their other degree — [Pursuit](https://pursuit.unimelb.edu.au/articles/how-australia-got-so-many-law-schools); [UQLJ 2025](https://journal.law.uq.edu.au/index.php/uqlj/article/download/17243/12342/79946)
- More than 10,000 students enrol in law degrees each year (a loose figure from a university news item) — [Bond University news](https://bond.edu.au/news/bond-university-announces-capping-of-law-student-enrolments)
- 79% of 2024 law and paralegal studies graduates were in full-time work, down from 85% for 2023 graduates (Graduate Outcomes Survey 2024) — [LSJ](https://lsj.com.au/articles/is-australia-producing-too-many-law-graduates/)
- Context: total higher education enrolments were 1,676,077 in 2024 (domestic 1,086,789) — [Department of Education, 2024 key findings](https://www.education.gov.au/higher-education-statistics/student-data/selected-higher-education-statistics-2024-student-data/key-findings-2024-higher-education-student-statistics)
- Seasonality: in one Melbourne subject the 2,500-word research essay is due during the exam period, which illustrates end-of-semester peaks (an anthropology-of-law subject; **2018 handbook**) — [Melbourne handbook](https://handbook.unimelb.edu.au/2018/subjects/anth30018/assessment). The Sydney LAWS3431 abstract is due in Week 5, with the essay due later in semester — [LAWS3431](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS3431/2026-S1C-PP-CC)

### Inferences
- **Current enrolment (estimate).** About 7,500–8,500 LLB/JD completions a year (CALD) × average time in the degree. Combined LLB takes about 5 years, JD 3 years, and part-time study takes longer, so an average of about 4.5–5 years is reasonable. That gives about **34,000–43,000**, plus non-completers. The 2015 figure (36,500) sits inside that range. Taking the higher 15,000 graduate figure (which includes legal studies) gives a broader pool of about 60,000+ students who sometimes meet AGLC. **Working figure: about 40,000 core LLB/JD students; about 10,000 new each year.**
- **Annual document volume (estimate).** 40,000 students × 4–10 documents a year ≈ **160,000–400,000 AGLC documents a year**, or about 200,000–600,000 runs with re-runs. At A$4 per document, the theoretical maximum is about A$0.6–1.6m a year at 100% penetration. A realistic 2–5% paying penetration gives about **A$15k–80k a year**. Under "A$4 for 20 uses", each payer pays about A$4 a year at most, so the same penetration gives about **A$3k–8k a year**.
- **Seasonality (based on typical Australian academic calendars; not sourced here).** Semester 1 runs about late February to June and Semester 2 about late July to November. Essays cluster in Weeks 8–13 and the exam period, so expect demand peaks around **late April to mid-June** and **late September to mid-November**, with troughs in January and July. Summer and winter intensives (like Sydney LAWS6252) add small off-peak demand. Today (1 November) is inside the Semester 2 peak.

### Gaps
- No current (2023–2026) national LLB+JD enrolment count. That needs the Department of Education uCube or Student Enrolment Pivot Table (by narrow field "Law"), or the current CALD factsheet; all were blocked here.
- No split of commencing LLB versus JD students.
- No university academic calendar fetched to confirm peak weeks.

## 4. Willingness to pay

### Takeaway
Australian students are under real cost-of-living pressure: a third expect expenses to exceed income, and 58% are worried about money. They face strong free substitutes, including a free open-source AGLC Word add-in (Obiter), library and database citation exports, university-provided ChatGPT Edu (now at UNSW and Sydney), and free citation generators. I found no survey of what Australian students actually pay for study software. Comparable pricing points: Grammarly about A$17–19 a month (unverified), QuillBot student plans US$20–75 a year (sources conflict), and CiteMe Pro at US$4.99 a month or US$14.99 a semester. A$4 per document is within impulse range for a high-stakes essay. A$4 for 20 uses is probably well below what students would accept.

### Inferences
- **Value anchor.** A$4 per document is about one coffee, set against an assessment worth 30–100% of a unit, and against the 1-mark-per-100-words penalty regime and graded citation criteria. For a time-pressed student the night before a deadline, A$4 is an impulse buy, and the free 3-correction preview supports that. CiteMe's US$14.99 a semester (about A$23) and Grammarly's about A$17–19 a month show students already pay A$15–25 for a semester or month of writing tools.
- **Annual spend at the current price.** At about 4–10 documents a year, a student who pays every time would spend about A$16–40 a year. That is comparable to one or two months of Grammarly, and plausible for a heavy user.
- **"A$4 for 20 uses" (A$0.20 per run)** is about 10–20 times cheaper per document than the current price and close to free. It would likely raise conversion but sharply reduce revenue per user (one purchase per degree). It also signals low value against the 30–100% weighting of the work. Pricing worth testing instead (all hypotheses, not data):
  - (a) Keep A$4 per document as the impulse option.
  - (b) A **semester pass at about A$10–15** for unlimited or about 10 documents, priced below CiteMe's US$14.99 a semester. This fits about 2–5 documents per semester plus re-runs; re-runs being free would remove the main friction of tracked-changes workflows.
  - (c) A **thesis or long-document tier** priced by footnote count (eg about A$10–20 for 150+ footnotes) for honours and HDR writers.
  - (d) A **journal or editor licence** for student law reviews.
  - Free re-runs of the same document within about 7 days would match how drafting works.
- **Substitution risk.** Free options (Obiter, database exports, free university ChatGPT Edu) cap what students will pay for basic citation *generation*. Aforecite's differentiation is *whole-document repair*: Ibid and "(n x)" renumbering, short titles, and bibliography, returned as tracked changes. ChatGPT and Obiter don't do this reliably across a whole .docx. Marketing should lead with that, not with "AGLC formatting".
- **Open-source self-hosting.** Aforecite's own code is public on GitHub, so tech-savvy students can self-host, but few law students will set up uv and API keys.

### Gaps
- No Australian survey of what students spend on study software or AI subscriptions, or how many pay for ChatGPT Plus or Grammarly.

## 5. Academic integrity rules on AI for referencing, and how much AGLC marks matter

### Takeaway
Policies are moving toward permitting AI in unsupervised (take-home) assessments. Sydney permits AI in all "open" assessments by policy, and UNSW has a category that explicitly permits "standard editing and referencing functions". Melbourne and Monash leave it to the subject, and treat AI-created or significantly AI-modified work submitted as one's own as potential misconduct. Aforecite only formats references, does not write content, and shows every change as tracked changes. That fits the "editing/referencing assistance" category, but students may still need to acknowledge its use where a unit requires it. Referencing is usually a graded rubric criterion rather than a fixed percentage. The common "5–15% of the grade" claim has no primary source.

### Cited Findings
- **Sydney:** from Semester 1, 2025, AI is prohibited by default in supervised assessments and permitted and encouraged in all others. From Semester 2, 2025, every assessment is classed as "secure" (supervised, no AI) or "open" (AI permitted, and AI use cannot be prohibited) — [Sydney news, Nov 2024](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2024/11/27/university-of-sydney-ai-assessment-policy.html); [Sydney academic integrity: AI](https://www.sydney.edu.au/students/academic-integrity/artificial-intelligence.html); [Cesare Giardito Substack commentary](https://cesaregardito.substack.com/p/sydneys-two-lanes-approach)
- **UNSW:** its six-category AI-use scheme includes "Simple editing assistance", defined as permitted use of standard editing and referencing functions in the software you use to complete your assessment (date of the post unclear) — [UNSW Education, "2 lanes or 6 lanes?"](https://education.unsw.edu.au/node/780). UNSW student guidance says spelling and grammar checks are usually acceptable because they don't change meaning, while AI features that generate or refine text may or may not be permitted — [UNSW: Using AI in university](https://www.unsw.edu.au/student/managing-your-studies/academic-skills-support/toolkit/ai). A separate report says UNSW plans "can / can't / must" categories (date unclear) — [iTnews](https://www.itnews.com.au/news/unsw-reveals-new-measures-to-deal-with-ai-cheating-628831)
- **Melbourne:** submitting work created or significantly modified by AI as one's own may be academic misconduct. Since Semester 1, 2024, commencing coursework students complete integrity modules that include AI guidance — [UniMelb Academic Integrity: University policy and actions](https://academicintegrity.unimelb.edu.au/staff-resources/artificial-intelligence/university-policy-and-actions)
- **Monash:** chief examiners set AI conditions for each assessment using approved wording, and students must acknowledge AI contributions — [Monash TeachHQ AI policy guidance](https://www.monash.edu/learning-teaching/teachhq/Teaching-practices/artificial-intelligence/old-version/policy-and-practice-guidance-around-acceptable-and-responsible-use-of-ai-technologies2); [Monash Graduate Research GenAI guidance](https://www.monash.edu/graduate-research/support-and-resources/guidance-on-generative-ai)
- **ECU** says rules vary by unit and tells students to check with their lecturer — [ECU legal citation guide](https://ecu.au.libguides.com/law/legal-citation). UniSQ's AI referencing guidance says each unit has different rules on generative AI use — [UniSQ AI for students](https://usq-qld.libguides.com/artificial-intelligence-students/referencing)
- **Australian universities offering AI referencing help themselves:** UNE's library runs an AGLC4 AI agent (SmartCite) — [UNE](https://www.une.edu.au/library/students/referencing-and-plagiarism/aglc4-smartcite); [UNE student experience blog, Jun 2025](https://blog.une.edu.au/studentexperience/2025/06/24/aglc-4th-with-ai-check-your-references-and-clarify-your-understanding)
- **Policy framing:** an analysis of Australian university AI policies finds misconduct narratives dominate — [JCU CADSI, "Guardrails or handbrakes"](https://www.jcu.edu.au/cadsi/our-impact-areas/communities/guardrails-or-handbrakes-how-australian-universities-frame-ai-in-policy,-and-the-dominance-of-misconduct-narratives)
- **How AGLC is marked:**
  - UWA's LAWS1112 rubric grades "citation of authorities and footnotes" in bands from "correct and consistent" down to "contained a number of errors" — [UWA AGLC4 guide / format examples](https://guides.library.uwa.edu.au/AGLC4/format_examples)
  - A Deakin assignment requirement quoted by the student society says "referencing must be AGLC-compliant" — [Deakin LSS](https://www.deakinlss.org/post/this-just-in-law-student-realises-they-shouldn-t-have-left-referencing-until-the-end)
  - Sydney LAWS3431 requires the current AGLC edition — [LAWS3431](https://www.sydney.edu.au/units/LAWS3431/2026-S1C-PP-CC)
  - A self-published blog claims referencing is worth 5–15% of the grade on most rubrics. This is a weak source and unverified — [reffinder blog](https://reffinder-y8ou.onrender.com/blog/how-to-avoid-losing-marks-referencing)

### Inferences
- **Helpful for adoption:**
  - Aforecite reformats existing citations and does not generate arguments or sources. Under UNSW's categories it most plausibly falls within "simple editing assistance" (referencing functions). Under Sydney's model it is allowed in any "open" assessment.
  - Output as Word tracked changes makes the assistance transparent and auditable, which helps with integrity concerns and with acknowledgement statements.
  - UNE building its own AI AGLC agent shows universities see AI citation help as legitimate.
- **Risks:**
  - (a) Melbourne-style "significantly modified by AI" wording, and unit-level prohibitions at Monash and Melbourne, could make cautious students hesitate. A short FAQ or acknowledgement template ("Citations formatted with Aforecite; no content generated") would help.
  - (b) Some teachers treat AGLC competence as a learning outcome in first-year legal research units, where automated formatting may be discouraged.
  - (c) Privacy and upload concerns about sending unpublished essays to an AI model may matter to cautious students and HDR candidates.
  - (d) Units that count footnotes toward the word limit mean footnote edits change the word count. Showing a before/after word count would add value.
- **Stakes.** Citation is usually a graded criterion within a 30–100%-weighted assessment rather than a fixed share. Errors cost marks through the "presentation/citation" band and through general impressions of quality. The "5–15%" figure should not be quoted without a primary source.
