AGLC4 signals: See, See also, Cf and Contra

An introductory signal is a word or phrase before a citation, such as See or Cf, that tells the reader how the source relates to the sentence it supports (r 1.2). In AGLC4, signals are never italicised, start with a capital at the beginning of a footnote and are lower case inside a sentence.

What each signal means

Choose the signal by asking what the source does for your proposition. In plain words:

  • No signal: the source itself states the proposition, or it is the source of a quotation.
  • See: the source supports the proposition.
  • See also: the source gives further support, in addition to sources already cited.
  • See especially: of several sources supporting the proposition, this is the most useful one.
  • See, eg,: the source is one example among several that support the proposition.
  • See generally: the source gives background on the proposition.
  • Cf: the source supports a different but comparable proposition, so it supports yours by analogy.
  • But see: the source points the other way.
  • Contra: the source directly contradicts the proposition.

How to write a signal

  • Not italic. Write See, not See (r 1.2).
  • Capitalised at the start of a footnote or a new sentence, and lower case elsewhere, such as after a comma or a colon inside a sentence.
  • No full stop after Cf or eg. "See, eg," keeps its two commas.
  • A new signal starts a new sentence. When a footnote moves from one signal to another, end the first citation with a full stop and start the next sentence with the new signal, capitalised. Sources under the same signal are separated by semicolons (r 1.1.3).
  • Ibid after a signal is lower case: "See ibid" (r 1.4.3). See Ibid.

Common questions

Are signals italicised in AGLC4?

No. See, See also, Cf and the other signals are written in ordinary roman type (r 1.2), unlike some other citation styles.

Is it "Cf" or "cf."?

Cf, with no full stop: AGLC4 doesn't put full stops in abbreviations. It is capitalised at the start of a footnote or sentence and lower case inside a sentence.

What is the difference between See and See also?

See introduces a source that supports your proposition. See also introduces further support, after the main source or sources have been cited.

Do I need a signal at all?

Not when the source states the proposition itself or you are quoting it. Use a signal only when it tells the reader something about how the source relates to your text.

What {{brand}} does

{{brand}} keeps the signal you chose: it never adds, removes or changes one, because which signal fits is a judgement about your argument. It writes the signal in roman type, capitalises it or lower-cases it for its place in the footnote, and starts a new sentence when the signal changes.
