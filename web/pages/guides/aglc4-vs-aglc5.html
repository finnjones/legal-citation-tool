AGLC4 or AGLC5: which edition should you use?

Use AGLC4. The fourth edition of the Australian Guide to Legal Citation, published in 2018, is the current edition, and the one Australian law schools generally require. As at November 2026 we know of no published fifth edition and no announced release date.

Is there an AGLC5?

Not yet, as far as we can tell. The Melbourne University Law Review Association, which publishes the Guide, has asked for public submissions on a fifth edition, but we haven't found a published AGLC5 or a release date. Its AGLC page is where an announcement would appear.

Until a fifth edition is published and your unit adopts it, follow AGLC4. If your unit outline names an edition, follow the outline.

The editions so far

  • First edition: 1998
  • Second edition: 2002
  • Third edition (AGLC3): 2010
  • Fourth edition (AGLC4): 2018

The change from AGLC3 you are most likely to meet

If you learned from older materials, or are reusing footnotes from an earlier essay, look out for "above n". AGLC3 referred back to an earlier footnote with "above n 1"; AGLC4 uses "(n 1)" instead, after the short form of the source (r 1.4.1):

Common questions

Is AGLC4 still current?

Yes. As at November 2026, the fourth edition (2018) is the current edition of the Australian Guide to Legal Citation.

When will AGLC5 be released?

We haven't seen a release date announced. The Melbourne University Law Review Association's AGLC page is the place to check.

Should I use "above n" or "(n x)"?

"(n x)". "Above n" belongs to AGLC3; AGLC4 replaced it.

Which edition does {{brand}} follow?

AGLC4. Every citation it writes follows the fourth edition.
